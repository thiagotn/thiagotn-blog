There's an ongoing debate in software engineering: **how technical does an Engineering Manager (EM) need to be?** The current trend points toward "increasingly technical" — the general sentiment in the community is that engineering managers should be hands-on, and the market direction confirms this.

This is especially true today because the boundaries between **Architect**, **Staff Engineer**, and **Engineering Manager** are increasingly blurred. It's often expected that a single person can wear all three hats as needed.

But does that mean you *need* to be technical as an EM in every situation? The original article by [Gregor Ojstersek](https://newsletter.eng-leadership.com/p/how-technical-should-an-engineering-258), published in the *Engineering Leadership* newsletter, argues otherwise. Below is a detailed summary of the key points.

## The EM's role is to do what's best for the team and the organization

This is a fundamental point to always keep in mind as an engineering manager:

> Your overall performance is judged by the success of your team, the impact you have on others, and the whole organization — not your individual contribution.

This automatically puts the EM in the position of an **enabler**: helping everyone do their best work possible and ensuring the team is successful.

The author describes much of the EM's work as **"filling the gaps of the team."** Concrete examples:

- If the team lacks good communication and cross-functional collaboration, the EM should take on much of that.
- If the team lacks technical seniority, the EM should fill that gap.
- If the team needs help contributing with tasks, the EM should do that.
- If the team needs help setting clear priorities and boundaries, the EM should focus on that.

And when you identify a gap, there are two options: **fill it yourself** as EM or **hire someone** to fill it. Both are valid — the choice depends on context, urgency, and resource availability.

## People manager vs. technical manager

You can't be the best at both. There's a limited amount of time every day, and it all depends on how you spend it.

**As a people manager**, you spend more time:

- Growing your team, helping each person develop;
- Unblocking the team when there are impediments;
- Communicating with stakeholders and aligning with leadership;
- Ensuring your team has everything they need to be successful.

**As a technical manager**, you spend more time:

- Reviewing architecture documents and technical specs;
- Reviewing code and contributing to the codebase;
- Actively participating in technical discussions and decisions.

Over the course of his career, the author lived both models successfully. He was more of a **technical manager** as Team Lead and Engineering Manager, and shifted to **people manager** as he grew into Head of Engineering, VP, and CTO — especially as his teams became more autonomous and no longer needed him in the code.

Both types work well, but it's important to understand that **being the best at both simultaneously is not possible**. If you spend more time on code and technical direction, you have less time for communication, unblocking, and growing people. And vice versa.

## Which to choose? It depends on what the team needs

There's a time and place for both — and this changes over time.

### When to prioritize people management

With AI accelerating engineering work, people work may be worth **even more**, because that's how you unblock the team to move faster. The problem is that many companies don't understand this and force managers to be more hands-on when the team needs something else entirely.

If the team is **already strong technically**, they don't need you in the code — they need communication, alignment, and unblocking. In this scenario, you need to be more of a **people manager**.

### When to prioritize technical management

If the team is **new and doesn't know the codebase well**, and you do, you can help a lot by being more technical — accelerating everyone's learning curve and reducing initial friction.

This brings us back to the starting point: **the EM's goal is to do what's best for the team and the organization.** There's no one-size-fits-all answer — there's the right answer for your context.

## The manager's role is to make their team better

The author recommends staying technical — you don't need to be the best coder, but it's important to **understand the details**. If you do, you can help the team much better with both people-related and tech-related issues.

It's also important to recognize that a team may need a certain type of manager at one point and another type later. To help the team in the best way, you need to **either change your management style or hire someone** to fill the gap.

Hiring takes more time; filling the gap yourself is faster — but requires flexibility.

### The "highly technical manager" narrative is a myth

> The narrative that goes around these days that you need to be highly technical as a manager is simply not true.

The author gives a concrete example:

> If you can enable your team of 5–8 engineers to be 20–30% more productive, that's *a lot* more valuable than just your own individual contribution.

And if you're doing this — multiplying the team's output — it's crucial to **communicate your impact the right way.** Individual contribution is more "visible" and "tangible" by default, but the work where you multiply everyone around you is **far more impactful.** Make sure everyone understands that.

## From people manager to technical manager

If you've been a people manager for a while and feel you've lost some of your technical chops, the author recommends investing in becoming more technical again — especially because, with AI-assisted engineering, it's **more accessible than ever**.

As mentioned, there's a growing expectation that roles like EM, Architect, and Staff Engineer should be close together. Understanding the problems your team faces helps a lot in making everyone successful.

Below are practical strategies to stay technical as an EM.

## 1. Separate your schedule into "manager time" and "maker time"

This is the **prerequisite** for everything else. If you don't do this, you won't be able to dedicate any time to staying technical. You'll end up in meetings all day, "busy" but not productive.

If you haven't already, **schedule focus blocks in your calendar.** The author recommends splitting your time like this:

**Maker time** — Your "head-down" time. Focus on strategy, learning, building, reviewing. No interruptions, no emails, no Slack. It's you, focusing on doing — and it's crucial for staying technical as an EM.

**Manager time** — The rest of your available time. Meetings, 1:1s with your team, your manager, and peers. Responding to Slack and email. Tasks that don't require long blocks.

These are two very different ways of working. If you try to mix them, you'll end up constantly context-switching and draining your energy quickly.

The author shares that, as a full-time CTO, he explicitly separated these blocks in his calendar — and that's what allowed him to continue contributing technically even in a senior leadership position.

## 2. Build proofs-of-concept

This is what the author did most as a full-time CTO. Whenever a business idea came up — his own or someone else's from the leadership team — he quickly started prototyping to assess feasibility within the system.

This helped him to:

- Have a clear view of what was possible and what wasn't;
- Assess how difficult it would be to develop that idea;
- Understand whether the idea made sense based on system trade-offs;
- Stay technical while primarily being a team enabler.

Additionally, if he didn't do this, he'd need to assign the PoC to someone on the team — which would take time away from building and cause him to lose understanding of the system as a whole.

> I highly recommend you include this as part of your process. And now it's easier than ever with AI-assisted engineering — you can quickly build solid prototypes, get explanations of parts of the codebase, and get a good idea of feasibility before something even becomes a priority for the team.

## 3. Review tech specs, architecture docs, and PRs

Participate in architecture and tech spec discussion meetings. It's a great way to:

- Stay up-to-date via discussions;
- Understand technical trade-offs;
- Hear how people think about different problems.

Additionally, using LLMs to get explanations on certain topics and decisions helps as well. If you're at a smaller company, these discussions probably happen ad-hoc, before or after the daily meeting — which is great. At mid-size or larger companies, such meetings are organized separately, and you might need to ask to be added.

Reviewing PRs is another excellent way to stay up-to-date — and AI can help a lot. If you don't understand a line of code, you can simply ask the LLM for an explanation.

But there's an important rule from the author:

> **Please, don't generate review comments with AI.** That's a big NO from my perspective. If I see that, the person loses a bit of credibility in my eyes. If you do a review, use AI to get the understanding and to learn, but make sure to write the comment yourself.

## 4. Contribute to the codebase — but not on the hardest tasks

Important: **don't take the hardest tasks**, because you'll be a bottleneck. If you try to take on the hardest and most important tasks for a specific project, you won't have the time to properly finish them or continue to support them — and that can be quite problematic.

Instead, the author recommends taking on:

- Bugs;
- Minor issues;
- Small improvements;
- UX-related improvements;
- Anything that's not going to be crucial for the project's success.

As an EM, the author took on tasks in the **legacy system** while the team worked on the new system that would replace it. It was useful for everyone: the legacy was maintained, the team was free to focus on the new system, and he remained technically active without being a bottleneck.

## 5. Build a side project

Last but not least — with AI-assisted engineering, you can build a quick MVP pretty quickly. (Of course, it depends on what you're building, but you can get something out a lot faster than it used to take.)

It's an excellent way to stay technical, especially if you build something you're **passionate** about. Do you like basketball, football, or volleyball? Do you like video games? Do you have a hobby and want to share your work online?

> If you build something you're passionate about, you have fun building it, and at the same time, you're also staying technical!

## Final thoughts

The author closes the article with a reflection:

> The question isn't whether engineering managers should stay technical. The real question is: *what does your team need from you right now?*

Stay technical — that's important. But at the same time, make sure to do what's best for your team and the overall organization. And communicate your impact the right way.

Individual contribution is more "visible" and "tangible" by default, but the work where you **multiply everyone around you** is far more impactful. Don't let that go unnoticed.
