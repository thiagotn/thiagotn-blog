> *Summary of the memo published by Terk Terkowitz, VP/Distinguished Engineer, Worldwide Amazon Stores, on October 27, 2026, shared with all software development engineers (SDEs) and software development managers (SDMs) at Amazon. The original text is available at [aboutamazon.com](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-software-engineer-role-guideline-ai).*

---

Amazon's engineering leadership — including Distinguished and Senior Principal Engineers from across the company — came together to revise and update the **role guideline for Software Development Engineers (SDEs)**. The central conclusion is straightforward: what matters is the **outcome**, not which tools are used. The company's most prolific engineers have demonstrated that neither the developer role nor their output is defined by the use of AI. We're now in the era of the **"Renaissance Developer"** — blending AI tools with human judgment and domain expertise to build entirely new things.

## The central question: how much to focus on AI?

As they developed the new version of the SDE role guideline, leadership faced an important question: **how much should we focus on AI?** Historically, Amazon has tried to avoid making the SDE role guidelines explicit about technology, reflecting the diversity of businesses Amazon is in and the work its SDEs do. This approach held through two decades of technology trends (the cloud, microservices, serverless, etc.) that reshaped the work SDEs do.

Within the Senior Principal and Distinguished Engineer communities, there was deep debate about whether **this time is different**. The conclusion: AI is changing the work of software engineering both **more quickly and more fundamentally** than these previous trends. What it means to be a software engineer — and what software engineering even is — is changing quickly and durably.

Still, leadership believes it's still early. Everyone needs to embrace this change and take the time to learn, or risk being left behind. AI will be at the center of software engineering practice, whatever future best practices look like.

## Focus on what doesn't change

The difficulty of defining, in a way that will stand the test of time, what good AI-powered engineering practices look like is real. Any definition written now will likely look antiquated by this time next year. Antiquated definitions run the risk of slowing progress, freezing engineering practices in amber while the world moves on without you.

To solve this problem, Amazon decided to focus on **what is not changing** as they evolved the SDE roles. Specifically: impact on customers, businesses, and teams. The outcomes that matter aren't changing, even if the inputs, tools, and techniques are changing faster than ever.

> "What's going to change in the next five to ten years? But I very rarely get asked What's not going to change in the next five to ten years? That second question is actually the more important of the two."
>
> — **Jeff Bezos**, 2012 interview with Werner Vogels

## How to measure AI usage — and the problems with input metrics

The conclusion to focus on outcomes made it easier to write the role guideline, but leaves an important question for SDEs and SDMs: how should you be using AI, and how should you be measuring AI usage in your teams and organizations?

Amazon believes it's best to measure — and be measured by — **long-term outcomes**. But these outcomes can be time-delayed, difficult to measure, and hard to isolate from other trends. In some cases, the company has used **input metrics** instead: token usage, AI tool adoption, or **throughput metrics** like code changes and reviews.

Leadership is aware of how deeply flawed these metrics are, primarily because they're disconnected from what matters. Using these metrics as a target (instead of a measure) can drive negative outcomes:

- **Increasing costs**: "tokenmaxxing" is at odds with frugality
- **Increasing complexity**: measuring lines of code (LOC) is at odds with invent and simplify

On the other hand, Amazon has found that these input metrics **correlate well** with the outputs that matter and expects they will continue to do so for some time. This effect is driven both by increased productivity and delivery on teams that have embraced AI the most (called **frontier teams**), and by the fact that teams and individuals who are not adopting AI or increasing their output metrics are falling behind.

The recommendation is to pay special attention to the **10th percentile** (finding teams who aren't embracing AI effectively and understanding why) and the **90th percentile** (finding teams who are heavily using AI and understanding whether they're seeing delivery increases to match). Over time, working together, better metrics and smarter goals will emerge.

> "Good inventors and designers deeply understand their customer. They spend tremendous energy developing that intuition. They study and understand many anecdotes rather than only the averages you'll find on surveys."
>
> — **Jeff Bezos**, 2016 Letter to Shareholders

To paraphrase: good leaders deeply understand their team's work. They spend tremendous energy developing that intuition. They study and understand many anecdotes, and carefully track outputs — rather than only surface-level metrics.

## If AI doesn't help your work, that's a finding — not a failure

A crucial point from the memo: **if your work doesn't benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing**. Document why, share it with your community of peers, bring it to your manager, and work to find solutions. An engineer who can explain where AI doesn't yet help their work is demonstrating the judgment Amazon is asking for.

## The role of technical and organizational leaders

As a technical or organizational leader, part of your job is making sure your team or organization is using AI to the best effect to improve delivery for customers and the business. That requires:

- **Deeply understanding the technologies** — working with them hands-on rather than relying on what you hear
- **Production use** — having used these tools on your own production work, being able to describe a task where they helped and a task where they didn't, and explaining that judgment to your team
- **Careful balance** between exploring new and better ways of doing things and investing in doubling down on the ones you already have

The leaders having the most impact are those **doubling down on "learn and be curious"** — both spending the time they need to learn AI and making time for the people they lead to do the same. The returns on this investment are **disproportionately large**, with teams that followed their curiosity (appropriately balanced with deliver results and insist on the highest standards) seeing the most positive impact from AI adoption.

## Proximity to product and business

Amazon found that SDEs and SDMs who are **closer to their products and businesses** are able to take the most advantage of AI and agentic software development. As Amazon has grown, some ICs and SDMs have become less connected to their customers and business. The company wants to **reverse this trend**.

The recommendation is that ICs of all levels find ways to become deeply connected to their customers and their team or org's product direction. For SDMs, in addition, making sure they're close to customers and business, and helping the ICs in their organization do the same. As always, the scope and depth of this ownership increases as you become more senior.

## What really matters

The opportunity for software and software engineers has **never been larger**, according to the memo. Amazon will continue to evolve the SDE role over the coming years as AI's effect becomes clear. But what really matters is unlikely to change: **obsession with customers** and a **long-term focus on delivering results**.
